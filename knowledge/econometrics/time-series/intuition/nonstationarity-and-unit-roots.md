# Non-Stationarity and Unit Roots: Intuition

This page is the plain-language version of Week 3's first ideas.

## The basic problem

Week 2 gave us ARMA models for stationary time series.

Week 3 asks what to do when the series is not stationary.

A stationary series has a stable statistical environment. Its usual level and dependence structure do not keep changing just because time passes.

A non-stationary series does not have that stability.

## What non-stationarity can look like

A series can look non-stationary because:

- it trends upward or downward;
- it repeats a seasonal pattern;
- its fluctuations get larger as the level gets larger;
- shocks permanently move the level.

The time-series plot is the first place to look.

The ACF also helps. A stationary series usually loses correlation with distant observations relatively quickly. A non-stationary series often stays strongly correlated with values far in the past.

## What differencing does

Differencing changes the question.

Instead of asking:

> What is the level today?

we ask:

> How much did the level change since the previous period?

So

$$
\Delta X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}.
$$

If the level keeps wandering but its changes behave stably, differencing can reveal the stationary process underneath.

## Random walk intuition

A random walk is

$$
X_t=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t.
$$

Today's level is yesterday's level plus today's shock.

That means every shock becomes part of the future level.

If today's shock is +10, tomorrow starts from a level that is 10 higher than it otherwise would have been.

The shock does not automatically disappear.

That is why a random walk is not mean-reverting.

## Stationary AR(1) versus random walk

Compare:

$$
X_t=0.8X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t
$$

with

$$
X_t=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t.
$$

In the first process, the effect of an old shock shrinks over time.

In the second, the old shock stays embedded in the level.

The first is mean-reverting.

The second has a stochastic trend.

## What a unit root really means

For an AR(1), the characteristic root is

$$
z=\frac{1}{\phi}.
$$

When

$$
\phi=1,
$$

we get

$$
z=1.
$$

So "unit root" literally refers to a root on the unit circle.

For the simple case here, the root is exactly 1.

A useful correction is:

> No unit root does not automatically mean stationary.

Why?

Because a root can lie inside the unit circle. That is not a unit root, but it is still non-stationary because the process is explosive.

So remember:

```text
root outside unit circle -> stationary
root on unit circle      -> unit-root non-stationary
root inside unit circle  -> explosive non-stationary
```

## Why differencing fixes a random walk

Start from

$$
X_t=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t.
$$

Take away yesterday's level:

$$
X_t-X_{t-1}=\varepsilon_t.
$$

So

$$
\Delta X_t=\varepsilon_t.
$$

The wandering level has disappeared. What remains is simply the shock process.

That is why a random walk is a classic example of an `I(1)` series.

## What I(0), I(1), I(2) are saying

The notation is just counting how much differencing is needed.

`I(0)`:

> already stationary.

`I(1)`:

> difference once and it becomes stationary.

`I(2)`:

> difference twice and it becomes stationary.

The number is the minimum number of differences required.

## Why not difference everything?

Because differencing a series that is already stationary can create unnecessary dependence and make the model harder to estimate well.

The goal is not to difference as much as possible.

The goal is to difference only as much as necessary.

## Deterministic trend versus stochastic trend

A deterministic trend is a predictable movement through time.

For example, imagine a series that increases by roughly 2 units each year and wiggles randomly around that straight line.

A stochastic trend is different.

A random walk moves because shocks accumulate. Its future path is not a fixed line that was already determined by time.

That difference becomes important in unit-root testing.

## One-sentence explanations

**Non-stationary:** the statistical behavior of the series changes over time.

**First difference:** the change from one period to the next.

**Unit root:** a characteristic root on the unit circle, with the basic AR(1) random walk giving root 1.

**I(1):** the level is non-stationary, but its first difference is stationary.

**Mean reversion:** shocks gradually lose their effect and the process moves back toward its normal level.

**Stochastic trend:** a trend generated by accumulated random shocks rather than a fixed deterministic time path.
